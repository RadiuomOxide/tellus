# Planetary Stress Observatory: Feasibility and architecture brief

The Planetary Stress Observatory is **technically feasible, scientifically defensible, and addresses a real credibility gap** — but only if scoped honestly. Roughly 70% of its raw value can already be reconstructed by an expert opening five tabs (NOAA SWPC, USGS, Smithsonian GVP, Windy, COMET-LiCS). The novel contribution is unifying space, sky, and ground onto a single time-synced 3D globe with curated event "case files" — explicitly as the **scientifically anchored alternative to fragmented official tools and conspiracy-coded popular ones** like SuspiciousObservers. The Hunga Tonga 2022 eruption — a rare event registered simultaneously by every layer this platform proposes — is the calibrating case that justifies the architecture.

The recommended stack is **React + TypeScript + CesiumJS (via Resium) + deck.gl** for the frontend, **FastAPI + TimescaleDB/PostGIS + Redis Streams + TiTiler** for the backend, and **PWA wrapped in Capacitor** for iOS and Android. CesiumJS is the only library purpose-built for time-dynamic 3D globes spanning decades of data; once that choice is made, everything else cascades from staying in the JS/web ecosystem. The hardest constraints are honest data gaps (no global free real-time lightning outside the Americas, no machine-readable Schumann API, no InSAR before 2014) rather than technical blockers.

## Free data sources that actually work

The genuinely usable, free, real-time foundation is narrower than it first appears. **NOAA SWPC's `services.swpc.noaa.gov`** is the keystone: dozens of JSON endpoints update at 1-minute cadence with no auth, no rate limit, no key — covering solar wind (mag-1-day.json, plasma-1-day.json), planetary K-index, GOES X-ray flux, OVATION aurora probability grids, alerts, and 3-day forecasts. **NASA DONKI** via `api.nasa.gov/DONKI/` provides the event catalog (CMEs, flares, geomagnetic storms, SEPs) with a free 1,000-request/hour key; treat as catalog quality rather than operational. **USGS Earthquake GeoJSON feeds** (`earthquake.usgs.gov/earthquakes/feed/v1.0/summary/all_hour.geojson` and variants) update every 1–5 minutes with no auth; **EMSC's WebSocket** at `wss://www.seismicportal.eu/standing_order/websocket` provides sub-minute push and is the lowest-latency option in the entire stack. **Smithsonian GVP** exposes a WFS GeoJSON endpoint for ~1,215 Holocene volcanoes. **USGS Geomagnetism Web Service** (`geomag.usgs.gov/ws/data/`) delivers 1-second magnetometer data from US observatories with no key — this is the single most reliable real-time ground geomagnetic feed.

For lightning, **only NOAA GOES GLM via AWS S3** (`s3://noaa-goes19/GLM-L2-LCFA/`) provides truly free real-time global-class data — but coverage is **Americas-only**, ~54°N to 54°S. **Blitzortung explicitly forbids third-party app use** of its raw data per its official terms; planning around it would violate the project's open-data ethic. **WWLLN restricts real-time access to sensor hosts** (paid otherwise). For Europe/Asia/Africa lightning, there is no free real-time replacement — note this gap honestly.

The Schumann resonance situation is bleak: **no public source provides a structured, machine-readable API**. Tomsk State University, HeartMath GCMS, and Cumiana publish PNG/JPG spectrograms only. HeartMath's hardware data is real but its surrounding "global consciousness" interpretive framework is fringe and should not be uncritically embedded. **The honest solution is to compute Schumann-band power in-house** by spectral analysis of the USGS 1-Hz magnetometer streams in the 7–40 Hz band — this gives a real, controlled, rights-clean signal. Infrasound is similarly restricted: **CTBTO IMS data is treaty-confidential**, and access via the vDEC research program forbids public redistribution. Use IRIS FDSN-WS BDF channels for the regional networks that are open, and disclose that global IMS data is unavailable.

For ground deformation, **InSAR is fundamentally not real-time**: best-case Sentinel-1 latency is ~24 hours after acquisition, and processing adds days. The **COMET-LiCS portal** pre-processes Sentinel-1 globally and is the most browser-friendly source; **ASF HyP3** offers free on-demand processing (10,000 credits/month) but jobs take minutes to hours; **EGMS** (European Ground Motion Service) provides pan-European mm-precision deformation but updates **annually**, not in real time. Sentinel-1B failed in December 2021 and Sentinel-1C/D launched in 2024–2025, so the 2022–2024 InSAR archive has 12-day rather than 6-day revisit — a real artifact users will see in time series. For GNSS, **Nevada Geodetic Lab (`geodesy.unr.edu`)** is by far the easiest source: ~13,000 stations globally, predictable URLs, no auth, daily and rapid solutions. EarthScope's own services now require IdM login.

## What the science actually says

The literature divides cleanly into established physics, contested research, and pseudoscience — and the platform's framing must respect those boundaries.

**Established and not seriously disputed:** Solar X-ray flares lower the ionospheric D-region and shift Schumann resonance frequencies by tenths of a Hertz on minute-to-hour timescales (Sátori, Williams, Nickolaenko, Hayakawa groups; Dyrda et al. 2015). InSAR routinely measures coseismic and postseismic deformation at cm-mm scales (Massonnet & Feigl 1998 onward). **Approximately half of erupting volcanoes show measurable pre-eruptive deformation** (Biggs et al. 2014, Ebmeier et al. 2018) — Sierra Negra 2018 showed ~6.5 m of pre-eruption uplift over years. Slow-slip events on subduction zones are visible in GPS and increasingly InSAR. Earthquakes generate measurable ionospheric TEC perturbations *after* rupture via acoustic-gravity waves.

**Active and contested:** Several peer-reviewed groups (De Santis, Marchetti, Akhoondzadeh, Pulinets) using ESA Swarm data report statistical concentrations of ionospheric anomalies in the weeks before M5.5+ earthquakes. Equally peer-reviewed reanalyses (Masci & Thomas 2015; Love & Thomas 2013; Geller, Jackson, Kagan & Mulargia 1997 *Science*) attribute these to ordinary geomagnetic activity, instrument artifacts, or retrospective Dobrovolsky-zone selection. **The USGS official position is unambiguous: no causal link between space weather and earthquakes has been demonstrated, and earthquakes cannot currently be predicted.** This is the most fraught area in the project; the platform must visualize co-occurrence without implying causation.

**Pseudoscience to flag and exclude:** Freund's "p-hole" hypothesis as a unified earthquake-precursor mechanism is not accepted by mainstream seismology (the lab physics is real; the field-scale extrapolation is not). Frank Hoogerbeets' planetary-alignment claims are flatly rejected by USGS. Claims that the 7.83 Hz Schumann fundamental drives human consciousness or "ascension" are numerological — the magnetic field strength is many orders of magnitude below biological thresholds. Ben Davidson / SuspiciousObservers' "electroquakes," imminent solar micronova, and crustal pole-flip claims have been documented as pseudoscience by RationalWiki and journalistic exposés; the platform should be **explicitly the credible alternative to that audience**, not adjacent to it.

**Hunga Tonga is the showcase event** that justifies the entire architecture. On 15 January 2022 at 04:14 UTC, the eruption produced a Lamb wave at ~318 m/s that **circumnavigated Earth at least four times** (Wright et al. *Nature* 2022; Matoza et al. *Science* 2022); meteotsunamis arriving ahead of conventional water-wave tsunamis (Kubota et al. *Science* 2022); a giant ionospheric TEC depletion lasting ~16 hours (Astafyeva et al.; Themens et al. *GRL* 2022); ~590,000 lightning strikes recorded by Vaisala GLD360, the **greatest concentration ever measured**; and a 2–6× Schumann resonance amplitude increase observed simultaneously at stations in France, Italy, Russia, Japan, and Antarctica (Nickolaenko et al. *JASTP* 2022; Mezentsev et al. *JGR* 2023). Every layer the Observatory proposes registered Hunga Tonga together. This is the strongest scientific argument for the platform's existence and should be the default "case file" loaded on first visit.

## The competitive gap

The closest existing analog is the **Smithsonian "Eruptions, Earthquakes, Emissions" (E3)** interactive at `volcano.si.edu/E3/`, which is built on **CesiumJS** and overlays earthquakes, eruptions, and SO₂ emissions on a 3D globe with time animation. It's three layers, not real-time, and lacks both space weather and ground-deformation. **NASA Worldview** comes closest in layer breadth (1,200+ imagery layers, near-real-time, with a Timeline Availability Panel showing per-layer data presence) but is 2D-only, lacks space-weather indices, and offers no temporal-correlation affordances. **Earth Nullschool** sets the aesthetic standard with particle-flow vector fields on a 3D globe and historical date scrubbing via URL, but has no event annotation or point data. **Windy.com** is the polished commercial benchmark — 50+ weather layers, side-by-side model comparison, optional 3D globe — but is atmosphere-centric. **NOAA SWPC** is authoritative but visually fragmented, with 1990s-era plots scattered across product pages. **COMET-LiCS** and **EGMS Explorer** handle InSAR well for technical users but are download portals, not browsing tools.

**Six things genuinely don't exist:**

- A single coherent temporal-spatial frame across heliospheric, atmospheric-electrical, and ground-response systems on the same scrubber and globe.
- Event-anchored "case file" mode with curated, pre-loaded, scientifically annotated playthroughs of canonical events (Hunga Tonga, Türkiye 2023, Halloween 2003, May 2024 Gannon storm).
- Honest uncertainty framing that visually correlates without claiming causation, citing primary literature on hover, and distinguishing established coupling from open questions.
- A credibility-coded alternative for the audience currently consuming pseudoscientific multi-system framings.
- Particle-flow space-weather rendering on the same engine as terrestrial layers, showing IMF and solar wind streaming into the magnetosphere as a coherent vector field in the Earth view.
- Multi-track event timeline handling dramatically different cadences (1-second magnetometers to 6-day InSAR to weekly volcanic reports) in one chrome.

**What is not novel and shouldn't be over-claimed:** 3D globe rendering (Cesium has commoditized this), real-time earthquake plotting, aurora forecasting, InSAR fringe visualization, and especially any "solar-seismic correlation engine" framed as predictive — that has no scientific basis and would put the project on the wrong side of the credibility line.

## The recommended stack

The decisive technical choice is the renderer, and **CesiumJS is the only library that satisfies the requirements**: WGS84 sub-cm precision, terrain and 3D Tiles, a first-class time `Clock` and CZML for time-dynamic entities, decade-spanning timelines, and an Apache 2.0 license. Three.js with three-globe or globe.gl produces beautiful "data viz globes" but reinvents geodesy and lacks terrain/CRS; **deck.gl's GlobeView is still labeled experimental** (no pitch, no high-precision zoom past 12, lnglat-only); MapLibre's globe projection is a re-projected 2D map without true 3D; Google Earth Engine is a server-side compute platform, not a frontend; Leaflet is 2D and disqualified.

The right architecture is **CesiumJS owning the camera, terrain, and time clock, with deck.gl drawing high-cardinality overlays** (100k+ lightning strikes, GPS displacement vectors, earthquake clouds) on top via GPU-accelerated layers. **MapLibre GL JS** serves as a 2D fallback mode reading the same PMTiles basemaps — important because cartographic research consistently shows users *enjoy* 3D more but *perform tasks better* in 2D (Smallman & John 2005). Default to 3D, offer one-click Equal Earth or Patterson 2D for analytical work.

For the backend, **Python FastAPI is the right choice** despite Node.js being viable — the entire geospatial and heliophysics scientific ecosystem (rasterio, GDAL, xarray, pystac, hyp3-sdk, sunpy, obspy, asf-search) lives in Python, and TiTiler itself is FastAPI. **PostgreSQL 17 + TimescaleDB + PostGIS + pgstac in a single instance** handles all point/event data with hypertables, ≥90% columnar compression after 30 days, and continuous aggregates that make 20-year Halloween 2003 replay tractable. **Cloudflare R2 with zero egress** stores Cloud-Optimized GeoTIFFs and PMTiles archives — a planet's worth of vector basemap costs ~$3/month. **TiTiler on AWS Lambda behind CloudFront** serves COGs dynamically with on-the-fly colormaps for InSAR phase rendering. **Static pre-tiled XYZ** for canonical historical events avoids cold starts.

For streaming, **Server-Sent Events backed by Redis Streams** is the right default: simpler than WebSocket, HTTP-native, auto-reconnect built in, with `Last-Event-ID` replay on reconnection. Add WebSocket only if filtered subscriptions become necessary. The fan-out pattern is ingestion workers polling each upstream source on its native cadence, normalizing to a canonical event schema, writing durably to Timescale, and publishing to Redis pub/sub channels per layer. Don't try to align 1-minute solar wind with irregular earthquakes and 6-day Sentinel-1 into a single timestream — keep separate tracks per layer with their own update frequencies, and let the timeline scrubber query each track independently at the playhead.

For mobile, the constraint is hard: **CesiumJS does not run inside React Native or Flutter** — it requires a real browser with WebGL2, DOM, and a `<canvas>`. Once Cesium is the renderer, the mobile question collapses to *how do we host a WebView containing it?* The answer is **PWA first (works fully on Android, second-class on iOS), then Capacitor wrapper for App Store and Play Store distribution** with native plugins for push, geolocation, and background fetch. WKWebView is meaningfully slower than mobile Safari for heavy WebGL — mitigate with lower terrain LOD on mobile, `requestRenderMode=true`, aggressive entity culling, and deck.gl off the critical path. React Native would force a renderer rewrite losing terrain, 3D Tiles, and CZML; Flutter's web build has known canvas performance issues; native Swift/Kotlin triples cost; Unity/Unreal with Cesium for Unity is overkill. The phased path is **PWA in months 0–6, Capacitor wrap in months 6–9, selectively native only if usage data demands it**.

## Data architecture and historical replay

The ingestion-to-serving flow is straightforward once data realities are accepted. Workers poll each REST source on its cadence (SWPC every minute, USGS GeoJSON every 30–60 s, Smithsonian GVP daily), subscribe to push sources (EMSC WebSocket, GFZ GEOFON SeedLink), and run async jobs against ASF HyP3 for on-demand InSAR. Each event normalizes to `{id, source, layer, t, geom, magnitude, payload}` and writes to the `events` Timescale hypertable with PostGIS geography indexing and a continuous aggregate at 5-minute buckets for fast scrubbing at zoom-out. Raster InSAR products convert via GDAL to validated COGs in R2, indexed in pgstac, and served dynamically by TiTiler with Crameri Scientific Colour Maps (`batlow` for magnitude, `vik` for diverging uplift/subsidence — these are perceptually uniform and colorblind-safe, used by COMET-LiCS itself).

**Historical replay coverage is honestly uneven** and the UI must reflect this rather than fabricate it. NASA's OMNI HRO archive at NASA SPDF holds 1-minute solar wind data **back to 1995** (hourly to November 1963), which fully covers the Halloween 2003 storms with ACE and Wind data; ANSS ComCat earthquakes go back comprehensively to 1973 (M≥5.5+ pre-1973); INTERMAGNET geomagnetic data covers Halloween 2003. **But Sentinel-1 InSAR does not exist before April 2014, WWLLN lightning before 2004, and Schumann archives are fragmentary before ~2014.** For Halloween 2003, the platform should display solar wind, X-ray flux, Kp/Dst, magnetometers, and earthquakes — and label other layers "Data not available for this date" rather than fake coverage. NASA Worldview's Timeline Availability Panel is the proven UX pattern for this: colored bars per layer showing where data exists, integrated into the scrubber.

Curated event bookmarks should anchor the timeline: **Hunga Tonga (15 Jan 2022, 04:14:45 UTC), Türkiye–Syria M7.8 (6 Feb 2023, 01:17:34 UTC), Halloween Storms (28 Oct–4 Nov 2003), Tohoku (11 Mar 2011, 05:46 UTC), May 2024 Gannon storm**. Each opens a pre-configured globe state with a multi-paragraph science panel citing primary literature.

## Design principles for honest visualization

The three layers must be visually differentiated by **visual variable, not just hue**. Space weather as **animated particle/flow fields** in a sun-side yellow/orange palette, drawing on Earth Nullschool's particle technique. Atmospheric electrical activity as **ephemeral glyphs** — bursting points fading over 20 minutes for lightning, extruded shells at ~300 km altitude for ionospheric TEC. Ground deformation and seismicity as **persistent geometric markers** — sized circles for earthquakes, focal-mechanism beachballs, draped InSAR fringe overlays. Z-stacking by physical altitude is intuitive and CesiumJS supports it natively: IMF/solar wind on outer halo, TEC at ionospheric height, lightning at troposphere, seismicity at surface, InSAR draped on terrain. Use **Crameri Scientific Colour Maps** (`batlow`, `vik`, `roma`) for sequential and diverging quantitative scales; **ColorBrewer Set2** for qualitative event categories; **never use jet/rainbow**. Each active layer gets its own legend color-coded to a side-panel layer card; never merge into one legend.

For science communication, the proven patterns are **Bartosz Ciechanowski's interactive explainers** (every concept gets its own sandbox; sliders directly next to the diagram; reader controls pace), **NYT-style scrollytelling with locked map and animated data**, and **Distill.pub's principles** (multimedia, redundancy, layered detail, multiple linked representations). For the timeline scrubber, compose the proven patterns rather than inventing: single drag handle plus play/pause (Google Earth Timelapse), Timeline Availability Panel (Worldview), multi-track event lanes (video editor Gantt style with one row per system), bookmark pins (YouTube chapters), speed controls 0.25× to 10×, and bookmarkable URL state. A **logarithmic time-scale toggle** would be genuinely novel and is needed because event timescales span seconds (seismic waveforms) to days (CME transit) to years (volcanic inflation).

## Honest obstacles affecting feasibility

Five obstacles are real and must be addressed in scoping rather than wished away.

The **Schumann resonance data problem** is the most acute: no machine-readable public API exists, every credible academic source provides plots only, and HeartMath's interpretive framework is fringe. Computing Schumann-band power from USGS magnetometer 1-Hz time series is the only rights-clean, reliable solution — but it requires implementing spectral analysis server-side and is a derived signal rather than a canonical observatory feed. Document this clearly in the science panel.

The **lightning coverage gap** outside the Americas has no free real-time fix. GOES GLM covers ~54°N to 54°S Americas/Pacific; Blitzortung's terms forbid third-party app use; WWLLN is paid for real-time. The platform must either accept hemispheric coverage or note paid Vaisala xWeather/GLD360 as the only solution for global coverage — a deliberate scoping choice, not a bug.

**InSAR is not real-time and InSAR is large.** Sentinel-1 SLC scenes are 4–8 GB each; processing requires HyP3 (free, async, hours) or running a full InSAR pipeline. The Sentinel-1B failure (Dec 2021) created a 3-year period of degraded 12-day revisit before Sentinel-1C launched in December 2024. The platform's "InSAR layer" will realistically show pre-rendered frames from COMET-LiCS with **days-to-weeks latency**, not live deformation — set user expectations accordingly. EGMS adds annual-cadence European mm-precision velocities as a static base layer.

**The earthquake-prediction credibility line is the project's single biggest editorial risk.** Visualizing co-occurrence between geomagnetic storms and seismicity is defensible *only* with consistent observational verbs ("co-occurred with," "registered simultaneously," "responded to"), explicit USGS-position language ("no causal link demonstrated; earthquakes cannot currently be predicted"), citations to both the De Santis/Marchetti claim literature and the Masci/Thomas/Love skeptical literature, and a clear visual distinction between established coupling (volcanic→ionosphere via Hunga Tonga) and open research questions (solar→seismic). Get this wrong in any UI text and the platform becomes indistinguishable from SuspiciousObservers regardless of technical rigor.

**EarthScope/UNAVCO data is migrating behind authentication** through 2024–2026, and Apple's iOS PWA support remains second-class despite the 2024 EU Digital Markets Act fluctuations. The Capacitor wrap is the insurance policy against iOS PWA limitations; **Nevada Geodetic Lab remains the unauthenticated GNSS source** that should anchor the GPS layer rather than EarthScope's authenticated services.

## Conclusion

The platform's strongest foundation is **NOAA SWPC + USGS + EMSC + Smithsonian GVP + Nevada Geodetic Lab + COMET-LiCS + GOES GLM (Americas) + computed Schumann from USGS magnetometers** — all genuinely free, all directly browser-consumable, with the Hunga Tonga case as the canonical proof that simultaneous multi-system visualization is scientifically meaningful. The genuine novelty lies not in any single layer but in the **time-synced multi-system frame, the curated case-file mode, and the editorial discipline to be honest about what is established versus contested versus pseudoscientific** — positioning the platform as the credible counterweight to both fragmented official tools and conspiracy-coded popular alternatives.

The technical risk is low; the editorial risk is high. CesiumJS, TimescaleDB, TiTiler, and Capacitor are all mature, free, and well-documented. The hardest engineering work is the multi-cadence timeline scrubber and the InSAR processing pipeline, neither of which is novel research. The hardest non-engineering work is writing every science panel and tooltip with verbs that distinguish observation from causation — an editorial review by an academic geophysicist before launch is worth more than any technical investment. Built with that discipline, the Planetary Stress Observatory becomes the missing scientifically anchored answer to a real public hunger for understanding Earth as a coupled system.
